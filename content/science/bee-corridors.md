A city park can be a valuable refuge for bees and butterflies, but the blocks between parks matter too. Researchers studying urban pollinators are paying closer attention to the small patches of flowers that make those green spaces easier to reach.

Roadside verges, school gardens and balcony planters may look disconnected to people. For a pollinator, a sequence of blooms can turn a difficult crossing into a route with regular food.

## More than a pretty planting

Native plants suited to a region can support a wider range of insects. Staggering bloom times gives pollinators food from early spring through late summer, while leaving a few undisturbed stems can provide shelter.

That approach also asks cities to rethink what a tidy verge looks like. Signs and clear edges can show passersby that a flowering strip is planned habitat, not neglect.

## Measure the connections

Counting species over time helps researchers see whether corridors are working. Residents can contribute observations, turning a neighborhood planting into a useful source of local data.
